A year ago, our two factories ran on spreadsheets, shared drives, and walking over to ask someone.

Today, both plants — NPI in Texas, mass production in Mexico — run on a manufacturing execution system we designed, built, and deployed ourselves. Every unit's full genealogy is one search away.

The interesting part was never the software. It was the build-vs-buy call.

Every commercial MES quote had the same shape: big license, bigger integration project, and a change-request process standing between my engineers and every process improvement. The dealbreaker wasn't the price — it was that our process knowledge would live in someone else's configuration.

For a factory whose core competence is INVENTING its process, the system of record had to move at the speed the process changes.

What actually made it work had little to do with code:

→ Bilingual (EN/ES) as a hard launch gate — a system operators can't read is a system they'll work around
→ Never breaking the other plant mid-deploy — two live sites, one database, every change backward-compatible
→ Earning the floor's trust station by station — replace the most hated paperwork first

An MES project is an organizational change wearing a software costume.
